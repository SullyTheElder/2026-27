The Scenario

Your school is hosting the Bulldog Esports Invitational in the gym: a regional gaming tournament with a live audience and a livestream online. Players, coaches, casters, referees, staff, and spectators will all be on the network at once.

If a player’s connection lags, a match could be delayed — or even decided by the network instead of the players. You’re on the event tech team, and it’s your job to plan the network.

Today is a design day: first you’ll make your best calls from what you already know, then you’ll learn the planning rules real network designers use, then you’ll check and fix your plan.

Concept: How Designers Plan an Event Network

Match the device to the job

  • Laptops suit people who type a lot, juggle several browser-based tools, or multitask.
  • Tablets and smartphones suit quick access to forms and dashboards to view and update data.
  • Gaming consoles, desktops, or high-performance laptops are needed where high frame rates and low input lag matter — esports, video editing, big data jobs.
  • Desktops or high-performance laptops with a wired Ethernet port are the right choice for management workstations and local servers, where reliability and steady access to admin tools matter.

Wired or wireless?

  • Wired for anything that needs high reliability, low delay, or big data transfers: gaming computers, management workstations, servers, and livestream equipment.
  • Wireless scales well for lots of devices. Event networks usually run separate wireless networks: a guest network (visitors get internet only — no access to internal resources), an internal network (staff with access to tools and platforms), and an admin network (secure, mobile access to administrative tools and data).
  • One wireless access point (WAP) typically supports 30–50 mobile devices (phones, tablets) or up to 20 computers/laptops. Signal gets weaker with distance and with walls or electrical interference, so big or oddly shaped spaces may need several WAPs.

Count the connections

  • Every wired connection needs a port on a switch. Plan for: the total number of wired devices, the ports available on each switch, the number and length of cables, and the number of switches. Think: what else in your design plugs into the switch besides computers?
  • A router connects separate network segments and directs data between them using IP addresses. Every device on a segment needs the router set as its default gateway to reach other segments or the internet.

Vocabulary

  • Endpoint device — a device people use to reach the network: a laptop, tablet, phone, desktop, or console.
  • Wireless access point (WAP) — a device that lets wireless devices join the network; it connects to the wired network behind it.
  • Switch port — one wired connection point on a switch; each wired device uses one.
  • Router — a device that connects separate network segments and directs data between them by IP address.
  • Default gateway — the router address a device sends data to when the destination is on a different segment.
  • Guest / internal / admin network — separate wireless networks for visitors, standard users, and administrators.
  • Network segment — a group of devices set apart from others on the network, with its own range of IP addresses.
