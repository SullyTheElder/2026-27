Concept: Where Connectivity Problems Come From

  • Physical/hardware issues — a loose, damaged, or unplugged cable, or a failed port. Indicator lights on a router or switch are your first diagnostic clue: steady/flashing green usually means normal operation, red or orange may mean a hardware issue, and an unlit light may mean a faulty port or an unplugged cable.
  • Congestion — too much traffic or a struggling connection shows up as high ping response times (over ~100ms) or noticeable packet loss.
  • APIPA addressing — an address in the 169.254.0.0–169.254.255.255 range means the device couldn't reach a DHCP server to get a real address.
  • Loopback addressing — an address of 127.0.0.1 (and nothing else) means the device isn't connected to any network at all — it can only talk to itself.

Vocabulary

  • Root cause — the underlying reason a problem is happening, as opposed to just its symptom.
  • Diagnostic tool — software or a command (like ping or ipconfig) used to gather information about a network problem.
  • APIPA (Automatic Private IP Addressing) — a fallback address a device assigns itself when it can't reach a DHCP server.
  • Loopback address — the address 127.0.0.1, which a device uses to refer to itself; seeing only this address means there's no active network connection.
  • Packet loss — data that was sent but never received a response; a sign of a weak or congested connection.
  • Latency / response time — how long it takes for a signal to get a reply; consistently high values suggest congestion.
